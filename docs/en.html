00
FOUNDATION

Learn to read the formulas first

Tell scalars from vectors, and understand magnitude, direction and units.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Plain letters r and v are numbers with size only; bold 𝐫 and 𝐯 carry both size and direction.

The length of a vector is written ‖𝐫‖. The length of position vector 𝐫 is the orbital radius r; the length of velocity vector 𝐯 is the speed v.

A dot above a letter means time derivative: 𝐫̇ = 𝐯; two dots mean acceleration: 𝐫̈.

Key formulas
r = ‖𝐫‖ = √(x²+y²+z²)

Turns the 3D position vector into a distance; x, y, z are its rectangular components.

v = ‖𝐯‖

Vector 𝐯 has size and direction; scalar v has size only.

r = Rₑ + altitude

Around Earth, radius is measured from the centre of the planet.

Variables & keywords
𝐫

Position vector: from the central body to the spacecraft

r

Magnitude of 𝐫, i.e. the orbital radius

𝐯

Velocity vector: how fast + which way

v

Magnitude of 𝐯, i.e. the speed

x, y, z

Components of the position vector

Example

A 400 km LEO is not r = 400 km; it is r ≈ 6378 + 400 = 6778 km.

Answer first: Two velocities have the same speed but different directions. Are the velocity vectors equal?

No. Vectors must match in both magnitude and direction.

01
FOUNDATION

An orbit is continuous free fall

See why a spacecraft neither falls straight down nor flies away in a straight line.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Earth's gravity always pulls the spacecraft toward the centre; meanwhile the spacecraft has a large sideways velocity.

It keeps falling toward Earth, but Earth's surface keeps curving away underneath it, so it stays in orbit.

The two-body model keeps only the gravity between the central body and the spacecraft; drag, third bodies and oblateness are ignored.

Key formulas
r̈ = −(μ/r³) r

Acceleration is opposite to r, so it always points toward the centre.

v_c = √(μ/r)

The ideal circular-orbit speed at radius r.

Variables & keywords
μ

Standard gravitational parameter; Earth ≈ 398600 km³/s²

r̈

Second time derivative of position, i.e. acceleration

Rₑ

Earth radius, ≈ 6378 km in this course

v_c

Circular-orbit speed

Example

A larger circular orbit has a smaller v_c. High orbit satellites move slower than low orbit satellites.

Answer first: Are astronauts in orbit weightless because there is no gravity?

No. Gravity is still strong; the spacecraft and astronaut are free falling together, which feels like weightlessness.

02
FOUNDATION

Ellipses and the orbit equation

Read apses from a given orbit, and know how θ marks a position along it.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

The central body sits at one focus of the ellipse, not the centre. Periapsis is the smallest radius rₚ; apoapsis is the largest radius rₐ.

Semi-major axis a sets the overall size; eccentricity e sets how squashed the orbit is. A circle has e = 0.

True anomaly θ is measured from periapsis along the motion: periapsis θ = 0, apoapsis θ = π. The orbit equation converts angular position θ into radius r.

The specific angular momentum vector 𝐡 = 𝐫 × 𝐯 is perpendicular to the orbit plane and stays constant in the two-body model.

Key formulas
a = (rₚ+rₐ)/2

Average of the two apsidal radii.

e = (rₐ−rₚ)/(rₐ+rₚ)

0 for a circle; between 0 and 1 for an ellipse.

rₚ=a(1−e), rₐ=a(1+e)

Recover both apses from a and e.

𝐡 = 𝐫 × 𝐯; h = ‖𝐡‖

Cross product gives the specific angular momentum vector; h has units km²/s.

p = h²/μ = a(1−e²)

p is the semi-latus rectum, linking angular momentum and ellipse geometry.

r(θ) = p/(1+e cosθ)

Orbit equation: given θ, how far the spacecraft is from the centre.

Variables & keywords
a

Semi-major axis: orbit size

e

Eccentricity: orbit shape

rₚ / rₐ

Periapsis / apoapsis radius

𝐡 / h

Specific angular momentum vector / its magnitude

p

Semi-latus rectum

θ

True anomaly: angle from periapsis

Example

For an Earth orbit of 400 × 1000 km altitude, first convert to rₚ = 6778 km and rₐ = 7378 km, then a = 7078 km and e ≈ 0.042. For the radius at any θ, use the orbit equation.

Answer first: What position does θ = π correspond to on an elliptical orbit?

Apoapsis. θ is measured from periapsis, so π rad is 180° around.

03
FOUNDATION

Energy, speed and the orbital clock

Understand why a spacecraft speeds up and slows down, and how to predict when it arrives somewhere.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Orbital energy is kinetic plus gravitational potential energy; in ideal two-body motion the total is conserved. An ellipse is fastest at periapsis and slowest at apoapsis.

Vis-viva gives speed only, not direction. Orbital period T depends only on a and μ.

On an ellipse, true anomaly θ does not increase uniformly. For timing we use the mean anomaly M, which does increase uniformly, then solve Kepler's equation for the eccentric anomaly E.

Only for a circular orbit (e = 0) can you simply write θ = nt.

Key formulas
ε = v²/2 − μ/r = −μ/(2a)

Specific orbital energy depends only on a.

v² = μ(2/r − 1/a)

Vis-viva: speed from current radius r and orbit semi-major axis a.

T = 2π√(a³/μ)

Kepler's third law: bigger orbit, longer period.

n = √(μ/a³) = 2π/T

Mean motion: average radians swept per second.

M = n(t−τ)

Mean anomaly M grows uniformly with time; τ is the last periapsis passage.

M = E − e sinE

Kepler's equation; usually solve numerically for E, then convert to true position θ.

Variables & keywords
ε

Specific orbital energy, km²/s²

T

Time for one full orbit

n

Mean motion, rad/s

M

Mean anomaly: the uniformly-running clock angle

E

Eccentric anomaly: helper angle for ellipse timing

τ

Time of last periapsis passage

t

Current time

Example

On a fixed ellipse a is constant. Near periapsis r is small, so 2/r is large and vis-viva gives a larger v; because the speed changes, θ cannot be used as a uniform clock.

Answer first: After one quarter of the orbital period, is θ exactly 90°?

Not necessarily. Only M increases uniformly; θ does not. Only a circular orbit lets you convert time to θ by simple proportion.

04
MANOEUVRES

How a burn changes the orbit

Build the core picture: change velocity at one point and you swap to another orbit.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

An impulsive burn assumes the burn is so short that position has no time to change but velocity jumps instantly.

Prograde accelerates along the current velocity and usually raises energy; retrograde brakes and usually lowers energy.

A radial burn pushes toward or away from the centre, changes the velocity direction strongly, and often rotates the apse line.

Key formulas
r⁺ = r⁻

Same position before and after the burn; superscripts −/+ mean before/after.

v⁺ = v⁻ + Δv

New velocity vector = old velocity vector + burn velocity-change vector.

Δε = v Δv cosα + ½Δv²

α is the angle between v and Δv; aligned burns at high speed change energy most effectively.

Variables & keywords
Δv

Velocity-vector change, the currency of manoeuvre cost

α

Angle between original velocity v and burn vector Δv

prograde

Along the current velocity direction

retrograde

Opposite to the current velocity direction

radial

Along the local vertical direction

Example

Make a prograde burn from a circular orbit: the position is the same but the speed is now too high for a circular orbit, so that point becomes the periapsis of a new ellipse.

Answer first: Does an impulsive burn instantly move the spacecraft to a different position r?

No. In the model, position is continuous and only velocity jumps.

05
MANOEUVRES

Rocket equation: what Δv costs in fuel

Convert the Δv computed from orbital mechanics into a mass penalty.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Orbital mechanics first tells you the required Δv; the rocket equation then tells you how much propellant that costs.

The mass penalty is exponential, so a little extra Δv can require a lot more fuel.

Isp measures propellant efficiency. Its unit is seconds, but it is not the burn duration; high Isp also does not mean high thrust.

Key formulas
vₑ = Isp g₀

Effective exhaust velocity = specific impulse × standard gravity.

Δv = vₑ ln(m₀/mf) = Isp g₀ ln(m₀/mf)

Ideal rocket equation; ln is the natural logarithm.

m₀/mf = exp[Δv/(Isp g₀)]

Shows directly that mass ratio grows exponentially with Δv.

m_prop = m₀ − mf

Propellant mass = total mass before burn − total mass after burn.

Variables & keywords
Isp

Specific impulse, seconds; not burn duration

vₑ

Effective exhaust velocity, m/s

g₀

Standard gravity 9.807 m/s²

m₀

Total mass before the burn

mf

Mass after the burn

m_prop

Propellant consumed by the burn

ln / exp

Natural logarithm and exponential, inverse operations

Example

With Isp = 300 s and Δv = 1000 m/s, mf/m₀ ≈ e⁻⁰·³⁴ ≈ 0.712; ideally about 28.8% of the initial mass is consumed.

Answer first: Does Isp = 300 s mean the engine can only burn for 300 seconds?

No. Isp is an efficiency indicator; the 'seconds' come from its definition, not from burn duration.

06
MANOEUVRES

Hohmann: the two most important burns

Fully understand the standard transfer between two circular orbits.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Hohmann optimality has strict assumptions: two concentric, coplanar circular orbits, two tangential impulsive burns, and a transfer ellipse tangent to both circles.

Outward transfer is prograde + prograde; inward transfer is retrograde + retrograde. The second burn circularises and cannot be skipped.

In the ideal two-body model the same path costs the same total Δv in either direction; 'returning is cheaper because of gravity help' is a trap.

Key formulas
a_t = (r₁+r₂)/2

Semi-major axis of the transfer ellipse.

Δv₁ = v_t(r₁) − v_c(r₁)

First burn for outward transfer: difference of two speeds at the same position.

Δv₂ = v_c(r₂) − v_t(r₂)

Second burn circularises at the target radius.

Δv_H = |Δv₁| + |Δv₂|

Total cost is the sum of burn magnitudes; signs must not cancel.

t_H = π√(a_t³/μ)

Only half of the transfer ellipse is flown.

Variables & keywords
r₁

Initial circular-orbit radius

r₂

Target circular-orbit radius

a_t

Transfer-ellipse semi-major axis

v_c

Circular speed at that radius

v_t

Transfer-ellipse speed at that radius

Δv_H

Total Hohmann cost

t_H

Hohmann flight time

Example

Standard solution order: find a_t → find both circular speeds → use vis-viva for both ends of the transfer ellipse → subtract speeds at each shared point. For inward transfer, reverse the process and burn directions.

Answer first: What are the two burn directions for an inward Hohmann transfer?

Both retrograde: the first lowers periapsis, the second brakes at the inner radius to circularise.

07
MANOEUVRES

Bi-elliptic and apogee ladder

Know why these two 'multi-burn' ideas are not the same thing.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Bi-elliptic has three burns and deliberately pushes the intermediate apoapsis far away; the cheap change at that low-speed point can save Δv, but usually costs a very long transfer time.

Remember the full thresholds: below r₂/r₁ = 11.94 Hohmann always wins; between 11.94 and 15.58 it depends on the intermediate radius r_b; above 15.58 a large enough r_b can make bi-elliptic cheaper.

Apogee ladder splits burns at the same periapsis over several orbits; in the ideal model the total Δv is not automatically reduced by splitting.

Variables & keywords
r₂/r₁

Target-to-initial circular radius ratio

r_b

Intermediate very-high apoapsis of a bi-elliptic transfer

telescoping

Velocity increments at the same point cancel each other out

coast time

Time spent coasting without thrusting

trade-off

Balancing fuel, time, engine capability and risk

Example

A small thruster that cannot do one large burn can use an apogee ladder; it mainly solves single-burn thrust capability, not magic Δv savings.

Answer first: For r₂/r₁ = 13, can you decide from the radius ratio alone that Hohmann is optimal?

No. 13 lies in the 11.94–15.58 middle zone; the answer also depends on the bi-elliptic r_b.

08
MANOEUVRES

Phasing: slowing down catches up

Master the most counter-intuitive, most-quizzed period logic.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Phasing is for the case where the orbit is correct but the timing is wrong. Predict when the target returns to the common burn point, then temporarily change the chaser's period so phase accumulates lap by lap.

Target ahead: at the common burn point make a retrograde burn. Speed drops instantly into a smaller orbit; smaller a means a shorter period, so the chaser completes each lap sooner and gradually catches up.

Once the phase change is complete, a second burn returns to the original orbit and matches velocity.

Key formulas
T = 2π√(a³/μ)

All phasing reasoning comes from the monotone relation between a and T.

a_phase = [μ(T_phase/2π)²]^(1/3)

Recover the temporary orbit size from the desired phasing period.

Variables & keywords
phase

Relative angular position on the same orbit

chaser

The actively manoeuvring spacecraft

target

The spacecraft being chased

T_phase

Period of the temporary phasing orbit

a_phase

Semi-major axis of the temporary phasing orbit

Example

'Brake and you instantly become faster' is wrong. The correct chain is: instant speed drop at the burn point → smaller a → shorter period → return to the meeting point sooner.

Answer first: To complete the same phase change in fewer laps, does the required Δv usually grow or shrink?

It grows. Less time requires a bigger period difference, so a stronger manoeuvre.

09
LECTURE 3

Off-apsis: Δv must be a vector

Separate 'change in speed' from 'change in velocity vector'.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

At an apsis the velocity is along the local horizontal; in a Hohmann transfer the two velocities are parallel, so speeds can be subtracted directly.

Away from an apsis the two flight-path angles usually differ, so the burn changes both speed and direction.

To find the flight direction at any point, get γ from v_r and v⊥ first; do not treat true anomaly θ directly as the velocity direction.

Even if the burn leaves speed unchanged, changing direction still requires non-zero Δv. The two non-Hohmann formulas below are just the generalisation of Hohmann; exams rarely require hand calculation.

Key formulas
v_r = (μ/h) e sinθ

Radial speed component: positive when moving away, negative when approaching.

v⊥ = (μ/h)(1+e cosθ) = h/r

Transverse speed component; combined with v_r it gives total speed and flight-path angle.

γ = atan2(v_r, v⊥)

Flight-path angle: velocity direction relative to the local horizontal.

Δv = ‖v₂−v₁‖

Definition: magnitude of the velocity-vector difference.

Δv = √(v₁²+v₂²−2v₁v₂cosΔγ)

Law of cosines for the two velocity vectors.

v₁=v₂=v ⇒ Δv=2v sin(|Δγ|/2)

Pure direction change.

e = (r_B−r_A)/(r_A cosθ_A − r_B cosθ_B)

Eccentricity of the general transfer orbit; A and B are the two connection points.

h = √[ μ r_A r_B (cosθ_A−cosθ_B)/(r_A cosθ_A−r_B cosθ_B) ]

Corresponding transfer angular momentum; Hohmann is the special case θ_A=0, θ_B=π.

Variables & keywords
γ

Flight-path angle, relative to the local horizontal

Δγ

Difference between the two velocity directions

vᵣ

Radial velocity component

v⊥

Transverse velocity component

r_A, θ_A

Radius and true anomaly of the old-orbit connection point

r_B, θ_B

Radius and true anomaly of the target-orbit connection point

Example

Two velocities of 4 km/s with directions differing by 60° require Δv = 2×4×sin30° = 4 km/s, not zero.

Answer first: When can Δv be simplified to |v₂−v₁|?

Only when the two velocity vectors are parallel, as in the tangential connection points of an ideal Hohmann transfer.

10
LECTURE 3

Apse rotation and Lambert chase

Meet two general manoeuvres; do not try to memorise the heavy solvers.

Expand ＋
Plain language first

Two orbits with different apse lines can only be switched with one impulsive burn at an intersection, because the burn does not move the spacecraft.

Adding a radial component at the old periapsis makes that point no longer an apsis, so the eccentricity vector and the apse line rotate.

Given two positions and a fixed flight time, finding the transfer orbit is a Lambert problem. The shorter the forced flight time, the more expensive the intercept usually is.

Key formulas
η = θ₁ − θ₂

Difference of true anomalies at the same intersection relative to the two apse lines.

Lambert inputs: r_A, r_B, Δt

Also choose the flight direction and revolution count.

Variables & keywords
apse line

Line joining periapsis and apoapsis

η

Apse-line rotation angle

Lambert problem

Two positions + flight time determine a transfer

rendezvous

Not just the same position, the velocity must match too

Example

Patient phasing may cost tens to hundreds of m/s; the lecture's one-hour forced chase needs about 9.43 km/s.

Answer first: Why can an apse-line transfer not burn at a point where the two orbits do not intersect?

An impulsive burn cannot teleport the spacecraft; the burn point must belong to both the old and the new orbit.

AFTER LEARNING / MEMORY DECK

Compress knowledge into memory cards after you understand it

This is not the starting point. Complete the course above, then practise recognising the model from the question stem.
01

Impulsive burn

Position frozen, velocity jumps

＋

r⁺ = r⁻; v⁺ = v⁻ + Δv. Whether the model applies depends on burn time / arc swept compared with the full orbit, not on a fixed threshold.

Question says short burn / instantaneous → think impulsive.
02

Rocket equation

Δv cost is exponential

＋

Δv = Isp g₀ ln(m₀/mf). High Isp saves propellant but does not guarantee high thrust. If g₀ is in m/s², Δv must be in m/s.

Question mentions mass, propellant or Isp → rocket equation only.
03

Hohmann

Two circles, coplanar, two tangential burns

＋

Outward: prograde on the inner circle, then prograde at the outer radius to circularise. Without the second burn the spacecraft returns along the transfer ellipse. Flight time is half the transfer-ellipse period.

Question has two circular coplanar orbits → Hohmann benchmark.
04

Bi-elliptic

Three burns, time traded for Δv

＋

Push apoapsis very high, make the cheap change at the low-speed point, then return to the target circle. Below 11.94 choose Hohmann; above 15.58 seriously consider bi-elliptic.

Usually saves little and flies very long.
05

Apogee ladder

Splitting burns at the same point does not save Δv

＋

All burns at the same periapsis telescope in the ideal model. The benefit is lower single-burn thrust; the cost is more coast time and more operations.

Do not confuse it with bi-elliptic, whose burns occur at different positions.
06

Phasing

Change the period, not the chase

＋

Target ahead: retrograde → smaller orbit → shorter period → catch up lap by lap. Waiting for a target behind is the opposite. Faster phasing usually costs more Δv.

Question says wrong place on the right orbit → phasing.
07

Vector Δv

Change of magnitude ≠ magnitude of change

＋

Off-apsis, Δv = ‖v₂ − v₁‖ and the flight-path angle matters. Even when v₁ = v₂, rotating the direction still costs Δv.

Question says off-apse / direction change → law of cosines.
08

Apse & chase

Intersections only; hurrying is expensive

＋

A direct apse-rotation burn must happen at an orbit intersection. Two positions + a flight time is a Lambert problem; a short forced intercept is usually far costlier than patient phasing.

Two positions + time → Lambert.

EXAM SKILL / TRAP BOARD

The six sentences most likely to trick you

  1. Slowing down does not mean long-term lag:retrograde puts you in a lower orbit with a shorter period, so you catch up lap by lap.
  2. Same speed does not mean zero Δv:rotating the velocity vector still uses propellant.
  3. The second Hohmann burn cannot be skipped:it circularises.
  4. Splitting a burn does not automatically save Δv:ideal increments at the same burn point telescope.
  5. High Isp does not mean high thrust:propellant efficiency and acceleration are different things.
  6. Altitude is not radius:Earth problems must first use r = Rₑ + altitude.
